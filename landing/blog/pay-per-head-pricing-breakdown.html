Pay Per Head Pricing in 2026: What 10, 20 and 50 Players Actually Cost

August 18, 2026 · 8 min read · By Tyler Kung

Pay-per-head pricing sounds cheap when it's quoted. Seven dollars a head. Nine dollars a head. Those are small numbers, and that's the point of quoting them that way.

The thing that makes them expensive isn't the rate. It's that the rate is charged per player, per week — so it multiplies by your group size and then again by 52. Below is what the real advertised prices work out to at four group sizes, using figures published by the vendors themselves in August 2026.

One assumption before the maths, stated plainly: these figures assume every player bets every week. Most pay-per-head services only charge for players who actually wagered, so a group with quiet members will pay less than the numbers below — scale them down by whatever share of your group actually plays in a given week. The comparison holds either way, because the structure is what differs, not the rounding.

Where the break-even actually sits

Run it backwards and the answer is blunt. At $7 per head per week, per-head pricing is only cheaper than $49.99 a month if you have fewer than two active players. At $10 a head, it's fewer than one and a half.

In other words, there is no group size at which per-head billing is the cheaper structure. That isn't a slight against the model — it reflects that you're buying different things.

The part most comparisons skip

You are not comparing like with like, and pretending otherwise would be dishonest.

A pay-per-head service is a full sportsbook operation. Casino. Racebook. Live dealer tables. A multilingual call center your players can phone at 3am. Human line management. Prop builders. If you're running an actual book as a business, those are not luxuries — they're the product, and $10 a head to not build any of it yourself is arguably fair.

Booki is not that. There's no casino, no racebook, no call center, and it caps at 50 members. It tracks picks against live odds, grades them automatically, and keeps the ledger straight. It also never touches money — no deposits, no payouts, no processing. You settle with your friends however you already do.

So the real question isn't which is cheaper. It's which one you actually need.

If you're running a book as a business, with players you don't know personally and volume that justifies a call center, pay-per-head is built for that and the pricing follows the value.

If you're running a book for twenty friends, you are paying somewhere between $607 and $867 a month for a casino nobody uses, a call center nobody phones, and capacity for a business you're not running. That's the case where the per-head model stops making sense — not because it's overpriced, but because you're buying an operation when you needed a ledger.

Verify this yourself

Prices move. Everything above was taken from vendor sites in August 2026, and the arithmetic is 4.33 weeks per month and 52 weeks per year. Before making a decision, check the current rate yourself — and ask specifically about the minimum weekly charge, since some services bill a floor regardless of activity, which changes the small-group maths considerably.
